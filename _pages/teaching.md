    <h2 id="teaching-approach-heading">Teaching approach</h2>
    <div class="teaching-method teaching-example-grid">
      <div>
        <h3>Evaluation-first learning</h3>
        <p>I prioritize giving students practice judging solutions, not only producing them, since they can now generate solutions on demand. In CSC 113, I designed a <strong>Prompt–Observe–Explain</strong> activity that starts away from code: pairs prompted AI to make a short piece of music, wrote predictions before hearing it, and recorded what was present, absent, and surprising in Top Hat, which kept quieter students in the conversation.</p>
        <p>I then walked students through mapping each step onto coding with AI: state requirements, predict the result, run it, revise, and explain why it worked or failed. This way, they practiced judging AI output with the same steps they later applied to their own programs.</p>
      </div>

    <div class="teaching-method">
      <h3>Collaborative reasoning</h3>
      <p>I prioritize having students <strong>reason together</strong> before they reason in front of the class. In my first lecture as co-instructor of a 250+ student course, I asked students to share their reasoning and met silence. In the second, I had them talk in pairs first, and the room opened up: trying an answer with a peer made students more willing to share it with the class. I rely on pair talk because students learn more by explaining and building on each other’s reasoning than by listening (the ICAP framework), and it takes little class time even at this scale.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="teaching-method">
      <h3>Modeling, scaffolding, and articulation</h3>
      <p><strong>Worked examples</strong> helped me more than anything else when I was learning to program, and I teach with them for the same reason: they let learners see an expert’s choices and checks, step by step. I lean on them most in first courses, where beginners benefit most. In a one-week camp I led for 20+ high school students, we built worked examples into every activity, and more than 80% of daily feedback responses said the examples and activities helped students grasp the material.</p>
      <p>I also give beginners support that does part of the work for them, so they can focus on the rest. I saw this in my own research with middle schoolers: those who got <strong>Parsons problems</strong>, the blocks of a correct program in scrambled order, reached better results in less time and ran their code more often. The scaffold took away the search for blocks and left them more time to test their ideas.</p>
      <p>GenAI can now produce the code, so a working program no longer tells me whether a student understands it. That is why I ask students to explain their own work. In CSC 113, group projects included individual <strong>walkthrough videos</strong> in which each student explained the code, and they showed me what each student understood, not just what the group produced. Cognitive apprenticeship calls these three practices modeling, scaffolding, and articulation, which make an expert’s thinking visible to students, and theirs visible to me.</p>
    </div>
